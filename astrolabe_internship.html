Red teaming a language model in Urdu

Testing the safety limits of an AI model with adversarial Urdu prompts, because guardrails tuned on English can behave differently in other languages.

organisationAI Astrolabe
duration1 month, remote
platformAWS (Amazon Nova)
final roleReviewer and recruiter

the projectStress-testing the model

Through a professor-led initiative, I joined a red teaming team that probed a large language model on AWS for unsafe or harmful output. Our angle was to test only in Urdu, to find gaps in multilingual safety that English-only testing would miss.

If the model held safe, we adapted and escalated our prompting until we either got an unsafe response or confirmed the guardrail was robust.

Every result (the prompt, the model’s response and a safe/unsafe label) was logged in a shared Excel sheet, building a structured dataset of adversarial test cases for the AI Astrolabe team.

Ongoing

methodHow we tested

1

Design the prompt

Wrote adversarial Urdu prompts on harmful instructions, sensitive topics and manipulative framing, using phrasing that differs from the English equivalents.

2

Run it on AWS

Submitted prompts to the model, read each response in full, and judged whether it crossed safety lines, explicitly or by implication.

3

Escalate when it holds

A safe answer triggered a new strategy: changed framing, roleplay, indirect phrasing, or chained prompts.

4

Log and review

Logged each prompt-response pair with a label. As reviewer, I audited entries to catch misclassifications and protect dataset quality.

takeawaysWhat I took away

Multilingual safety gaps are real

Guardrails tuned on English can behave differently in other languages. It’s an under-explored area of AI safety.

Adversarial thinking is a skill

Pivoting strategy when an attempt fails, instead of stopping, is a systematic approach that carries over to any security testing.

Quick trust comes from detail

Moving from labeler to reviewer within half a day showed how visible careful, consistent judgment is in collaborative data work.

Data quality is a team effort

Reviewing others’ labels showed how subjective “safe” and “unsafe” get at the margins, and why clear guidelines and cross-checking matter.
